Old Galvanized Pipes: Signs It Is Time To Replace Them

Galvanized steel pipe was common in older homes and it corrodes from the inside. These signs tell you when patching is no longer enough.

What Galvanized Pipe Is

Galvanized pipe is steel coated with zinc to slow rust. It was widely used for home water supply in the early and mid twentieth century. The zinc wears away over decades, and the steel underneath then rusts from the inside. The pipe narrows as rust and mineral scale build up, so water has less room to flow.

You can recognize it by its gray color and threaded joints. A magnet sticks to steel but not to copper or plastic, which gives a quick test on exposed pipe, such as under a sink or near the water heater.

Signs The Pipe Is Wearing Out

  • Low pressure at several fixtures, especially upstairs, while the main valve is fully open.
  • Rust-colored or brown water after the house has been unused for a few hours.
  • Pinhole leaks or damp spots that appear in one place, then another.
  • Flow that starts strong and slows to a trickle.
  • Visible rust or crust at fittings and unions.

Several of these together point to the pipe itself rather than one fixture. A single failure rarely comes alone.

Why It Matters Around Maywood

Maywood has a lot of housing built in the early and mid 1900s, so galvanized supply pipe is a real possibility. Your county assessor record lists the build year, and a house of that age that has never been repiped deserves a check.

If you rent, tell your landlord about low pressure and rusty water. These are signs the building may need work beyond a single repair.

What Repiping Involves

A repipe replaces old supply lines with new pipe, often copper or PEX. The plumber opens small access points in walls and ceilings and runs the new lines, then closes and patches the openings. Water is off for part of the day, and you should plan where to stay for a few hours.

Read repiping for the process and the choices of material, and ask for a written price before the work starts. If you have only one leak so far, burst pipe repair covers the section fix.

Do Not Forget The Drain Side

Supply pipe is only half the story. Houses of that age may also have cast iron or clay drain lines that roughen and crack. A camera can show their condition, and sewer inspection explains the process. Doing both checks at once avoids opening walls twice.

Copper Or PEX

New supply pipe is usually copper or PEX. Copper has a long record and handles heat well, but it costs more and can pit in some water. PEX is flexible plastic tubing that goes in with fewer joints and often with less wall opening, and it resists corrosion. Both are accepted by plumbing code when installed correctly.

Which one fits your house depends on access, budget and local code. Ask your plumber why they recommend one over the other, and ask what warranty comes with the work.

Planning The Work

Repiping is easier to plan than to live through. Ask how long the water will be off each day and which rooms will be opened. Ask who patches and paints the access holes, since some jobs include it and some do not.

Tenants should ask the landlord for notice, and landlords should give it. A written schedule helps everyone.

Take photos of walls before work starts and again when it finishes. They protect both sides.

Permits And Inspections

A repipe usually needs a permit and an inspection from the city building department. The inspector checks pipe sizes, support, pressure tests and shutoffs. A job without a permit can cause trouble when you sell or file an insurance claim.

Ask the plumber to confirm who pulls the permit and to give you a copy of the final sign-off.

A Home Test You Can Do

Run the cold tap at the kitchen sink and fill a quart jar. Hold it up to a light and let it settle. Brown or orange tint points to rust. Then open the hot and cold at the farthest bathroom at once and watch for pressure to fall.

If flow is weak at the farthest fixture and normal at the one nearest the main, pipe narrowing is a good suspect.

Do Not Ignore The Water Heater Connection

Old galvanized fittings at the water heater are a common failure point. Mixing dissimilar metals without proper fittings speeds corrosion. If your heater is being replaced in an older house, ask the plumber to look at the connections at the same visit.

What It Costs To Wait

Waiting is not free. Each pinhole leak inside a wall can stain drywall, swell baseboards and feed mold. Emergency repairs cost more than planned work and often happen at night. Pipes that fail one at a time can also cost a visit and a wall patch every time.

A planned repipe lets you compare prices, choose the material and schedule the work around your life. If you are not ready, ask the plumber to mark the worst sections and to tell you what to watch for.

Safety Steps For Old Pipe

Old pipe can fail suddenly, so these habits help while you plan a repipe.

  • Know where the main shutoff is and test it twice a year.
  • Do not hang weight or tools on old pipe, which can crack at threaded joints.
  • Do not drink or cook with water that runs brown, and flush the line until it clears.
  • Do not attempt to cut or unthread old galvanized pipe yourself, since it can snap and leak.
  • Call a plumber right away for a pipe that sprays or drips steadily.

Rental Owners And Buyers

If you are buying an older house, ask for a plumbing inspection and ask what pipe is in the walls. Galvanized supply is a negotiating point, since a repipe is a real cost. Rental owners should plan repipes between tenants when possible, because the work is easier with the unit empty.

New supply pipe must meet the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and California requires lead-free pipe and fittings for drinking water.
